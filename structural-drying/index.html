Of all the stages in restoring water damage, drying the structure counts most, and it is the stage most often botched by people without training. Once standing water has been extracted, a great deal of moisture is still held inside the structure: in insulation, concrete, subfloors, wood framing, drywall and other components. This trapped moisture must be scientifically removed through controlled evaporation and dehumidification to prevent the mold growth, wood rot, material degradation and odor problems that develop when structures remain wet.

The structural drying technicians at Pueblo Restoration are IICRC-certified and trained in psychrometry, the science of how temperature, humidity and moisture in air relate. That knowledge lets them work out where equipment goes, what temperature to hold and how air should move so evaporation is as effective as possible on every job. Plaster, concrete, hardwood and drywall each dry at their own rate, so each needs its own approach to get down to an acceptable moisture level with no added damage.

Daily monitoring is built into how we dry a structure, and it relies on calibrated meters, thermal cameras and thermo-hygrometers. We document moisture readings throughout the drying process, creating a detailed drying log that shows progressive moisture reduction in all affected materials. Those readings guide daily equipment adjustments that keep the drying as efficient as it can be. We call a job dry when every affected material meets the moisture standard in the IICRC S500 guidelines, and never just because a surface feels dry.

Why Structural Drying Is Not a DIY Job

Drying a structure properly takes science, and guesswork has no place in it. Simply placing fans and hoping for the best is the most common cause of secondary mold damage after water events. It involves daily monitoring, a calculation of the required grain depression (the capacity to remove moisture) and a working knowledge of the way materials absorb moisture and let it go. Without this knowledge, under-drying leaves moisture that feeds mold, while over-drying can damage wood and other materials.

Professional drying equipment is not a bigger version of what stores sell, it is a different kind of machine. A commercial LGR dehumidifier, short for low-grain refrigerant, pulls 10 to 20 times as much moisture out of the air as a residential one, while high-velocity air movers set up the exact airflow that speeds evaporation off structural surfaces. This equipment represents a significant investment and requires training to deploy effectively, both reasons why professional drying produces far better outcomes than DIY attempts.

How to Tell You Need Structural Drying

Catching these issues early helps you avoid costly problems and protect your property. Here are the most common indicators that it's time to call a professional:

  • After water damage, a meter shows elevated moisture in the floor, wall or ceiling
  • Beads of moisture on windows and cold surfaces after the water event
  • Blistered paint and lifting wallpaper on a wall that got wet
  • Drywall that is soft, swollen or shows moisture staining
  • Earlier water damage that was never professionally dried and now shows deterioration
  • Rooms that stay humid even with windows open and fans running
  • Baseboards or door trim that swell, warp or pull away from the wall

Maintenance Tips for Structural Drying

A little prevention goes a long way in avoiding expensive restoration emergencies. Follow these tips from our IICRC-certified technicians:

  • Each unit is positioned according to the moisture map, so ask before shifting anything
  • Every hour of run time matters, so resist switching the machines off for some quiet
  • Air movers need a clear path, so avoid stacking things on the equipment or in front of it
  • Make sure the technician can get in each day for monitoring visits
  • Let air movers and dehumidifiers run continuously, 24/7, until the technician removes them
  • The sooner drying starts after water gets in, the better things turn out, so do not wait
